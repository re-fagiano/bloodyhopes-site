The Smile as a Mask: Gallows Humor in Military History

Why armies that had every reason to despair kept finding ways to joke, sing, and smile — and what psychologists now understand about why it worked.

Term
Gallows humor — treating mortal danger as comedy material
Documented example
The Wipers Times, a satirical newspaper printed by British soldiers at Ypres, 1916
Psychological function
Creates emotional distance from trauma; recognized as an adaptive coping strategy, not a symptom
Related Bloody Hopes songs
Blessed Is the Man Who Smiles — The Montréal Smile · The Smile Inside the Square — Waterloo

Laughing at the Thing That Could Kill You

Psychologists call it gallows humor: the practice of turning a genuinely threatening situation into a joke, not because the danger has stopped being real, but because treating it as comedy material creates a kind of emotional distance from it. Modern research classifies it, alongside suppression and altruism, as a mature psychological defense — a sign of resilience rather than a symptom of denial. Soldiers have been doing this, in one form or another, for as long as there have been soldiers, long before anyone had a clinical name for it.

Napoleon's Grumbling Veterans

In Napoleon's Imperial Guard, the elite veteran infantry earned the nickname "grognards" — literally "grumblers" — for their habit of muttering complaints about army life within earshot of the Emperor himself, a privilege granted to no other troops in the army. Napoleon reportedly knew many of them by name, joked with them, and tolerated their grousing as a mark of the closeness between a commander and the men who had marched with him the longest. Memoirs from officers of the First Empire describe this same texture throughout the army more broadly: intense camaraderie, black comedy, and informal rituals that had nothing to do with official military discipline and everything to do with making the unbearable bearable one more day at a time.

The Newspaper Printed 700 Yards From the Front

A century later, the same instinct produced something more literal: an actual newspaper. In 1916, two British officers of the Sherwood Foresters, Captain Fred Roberts and Lieutenant Jack Pearson, found an abandoned printing press in the ruins of Ypres — known to British soldiers by the mangled nickname "Wipers" — and used it to print a satirical trench newspaper of the same name. Produced at times barely 700 yards from the front line, The Wipers Times ran twenty-three issues full of mock advertisements, parody poetry, and jokes about shelling, bad food, and trench rats, built almost entirely from inside references that only someone who had lived in those trenches would fully understand. Historians studying it today read it as a direct, documented expression of how soldiers used humor deliberately, as a tool, to hold together under conditions designed to break them down.

A Smile Is Also a Kind of Armor

Not every wartime gesture of warmth was a joke. Sometimes it was smaller and quieter than that: a look, a moment of unguarded kindness between people who had every reason to be enemies or strangers, offered simply because the alternative — treating every remaining moment as only about survival — was its own kind of defeat. This is the gentler cousin of gallows humor, working through the same psychological mechanism: refusing to let the war have the last word over every single interaction, even for a few seconds.

Two Songs, Two Smiles, Fifty-Five Years Apart

Bloody Hopes returns to this image twice. "Blessed Is the Man Who Smiles — The Montréal Smile" is set at the 1760 capitulation of Montréal and follows a veteran grenadier's smile at a frightened drummer, and a stranger's smile offered to a departing soldier in the street — small mercies that, the song argues, outlast the empires that produced the war around them. "The Smile Inside the Square — Waterloo" moves the same idea into the middle of open combat: a smile passed between two soldiers holding an infantry square against repeated French cavalry charges on June 18, 1815, a human gesture small and almost absurd against the scale of what is happening around it. Neither song claims the smile changes the outcome of the battle. Both insist it changes something anyway.
