The Smile as a Mask: Gallows Humor in Military History

Why armies that had every reason to despair kept finding ways to joke, sing, and smile — and what psychologists now understand about why it worked.

Laughing at the Thing That Could Kill You

Psychologists often discuss humor alongside mature defense mechanisms such as suppression and altruism because it can create emotional distance from a genuine threat. That does not make every joke evidence of resilience: humor may help people regulate fear and sustain group cohesion, but its meaning depends on context and it can coexist with avoidance, distress, or denial. Wartime testimony offers many examples of soldiers using comedy under pressure without proving a single universal psychological response.

Napoleon's Grumbling Veterans

Veterans of Napoleon's Imperial Guard became known as grognards, “grumblers.” Later memoirs and Napoleonic tradition portray their complaints as a tolerated sign of veteran status and familiarity with the Emperor. Individual anecdotes — including claims that Napoleon knew many by name or personally joked with them — belong partly to the legend constructed around the Guard and should not be treated as a uniform privilege enjoyed by every veteran.

The Newspaper Printed 700 Yards From the Front

A century later, the same instinct produced something more literal: an actual newspaper. In 1916, two British officers of the Sherwood Foresters, Captain Fred Roberts and Lieutenant Jack Pearson, found an abandoned printing press in the ruins of Ypres — known to British soldiers by the mangled nickname "Wipers" — and used it to print a satirical trench newspaper of the same name. Produced at times barely 700 yards from the front line, The Wipers Times ran twenty-three issues full of mock advertisements, parody poetry, and jokes about shelling, bad food, and trench rats, built almost entirely from inside references that only someone who had lived in those trenches would fully understand. Historians studying it today read it as a direct, documented expression of how soldiers used humor deliberately, as a tool, to hold together under conditions designed to break them down.

A Smile Is Also a Kind of Armor

Not every wartime gesture of warmth was a joke. Sometimes it was smaller and quieter than that: a look, a moment of unguarded kindness between people who had every reason to be enemies or strangers, offered simply because the alternative — treating every remaining moment as only about survival — was its own kind of defeat. This is the gentler cousin of gallows humor, working through the same psychological mechanism: refusing to let the war have the last word over every single interaction, even for a few seconds.

Two Songs, Two Smiles, Fifty-Five Years Apart

Bloody Hopes returns to this image twice. "Blessed Is the Man Who Smiles — The Montréal Smile" is set at the 1760 capitulation of Montréal and follows fictional gestures of mercy among soldiers and civilians. "The Smile Inside the Square — Waterloo" imagines a smile crossing between a British defender and a young French cavalryman attacking his square. Standard accounts do not record the massed French cavalry breaking an allied infantry square at Waterloo, and the song does not present its encounter as testimony. The French youth is a fictional casualty of a courageous but impetuous and poorly coordinated assault; the smile gives that tactical failure a human face.

Hear the contrast
“Not the smile of victory, not the smile of pride — but the small bright mercy a soldier keeps inside.”

At Montréal the smile is mercy; at Waterloo it is reckless courage remembered by the man who survives it.
