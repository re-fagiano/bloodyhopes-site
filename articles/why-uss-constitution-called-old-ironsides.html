Why Is USS Constitution Called Old Ironsides?

USS Constitution is made of wood, not iron. The nickname came from a moment in 1812 when British cannon shot appeared to rebound from the American frigate’s unusually strong oak hull.

The Short Answer

USS Constitution became “Old Ironsides” during its victory over HMS Guerriere on August 19, 1812. According to the history preserved by the United States Navy, a sailor saw British cannon shot fail to penetrate the frigate’s thick oak hull and exclaimed that her sides were made of iron. The name endured even though the ship was wooden.

The nickname is memorable because it compresses material, eyewitness reaction and national myth into two words. Constitution’s construction helped it survive the exchange; the crew’s reported cry gave that survival a name; and an American public hungry for good news turned the victory into a symbol.

The Battle with HMS Guerriere

Constitution met the British frigate Guerriere in the Atlantic, several hundred miles east of Nova Scotia. The ships closed and exchanged heavy fire at short range. The Naval History and Heritage Command’s account places the famous cry during this engagement, as British shot struck Constitution without producing the effect the attackers expected.

The fight was decisive. The Navy’s brief history records that Guerriere surrendered after roughly thirty-five minutes of close action. Her masts were badly damaged, and the captured frigate could not be brought safely into port. After prisoners and wounded men were transferred, the Americans burned the British ship.

Britain did not lose command of the sea in this single duel. Its navy remained vastly larger. But the psychological effect in the United States was substantial: an American frigate had defeated a Royal Navy opponent in a direct engagement early in a difficult war.

How Could a Wooden Ship Seem to Have Iron Sides?

Constitution was one of the original six frigates authorized for the young United States Navy. Its hull combined heavy construction with dense American oak, including live oak. The Naval History and Heritage Command specifically connects the ship’s thick white-oak and live-oak hull with its ability to withstand British fire during the encounter.

That does not mean every cannonball literally bounced away or that the hull was invulnerable. Sailing-warship combat damaged rigging, masts, spars and crews as well as hulls. “Old Ironsides” is the language of a witness and then of legend, not an engineering specification. Its historical core is nevertheless clear: Constitution’s robust wooden sides resisted the enemy’s shot well enough to astonish the men aboard.

From Battle Cry to National Symbol

USS Constitution’s career extended far beyond one battle. The Navy’s fact sheet credits the ship with victories over three British warships during the War of 1812 and service spanning decades. It is now the world’s oldest commissioned warship afloat and serves as the official Ship of State of the United States.

The nickname also helped save the ship in public memory. Oliver Wendell Holmes’s 1830 poem “Old Ironsides,” written amid concern that the aging frigate might be dismantled, transformed naval preservation into an emotional national cause. A phrase reportedly born in battle became a way to argue that the vessel itself should survive.
