Who Gets to Be a Hero: How History Rewrites Its Battles

One army rode to near-total loss following a garbled order and became eternal poetry. Another nearly lost a battle it's remembered for winning. Heroism, it turns out, is decided after the fact — and by whoever tells the story next.

Battle of Shiloh, April 6–7, 1862 — a near-disaster remembered as vindication

A Blunder That Became a Poem

On October 25, 1854, a confused chain of orders sent the British Light Brigade galloping directly down a valley into Russian artillery positioned on three sides, at the Battle of Balaclava during the Crimean War. The order had passed from Lord Raglan through a messenger, Captain Nolan, to Lord Lucan, who quarreled with Nolan over what exactly it meant; Nolan was killed before the confusion could be resolved, and Lord Cardigan led roughly 660 cavalrymen forward on the interpretation that was left standing. Of that number, an estimated 247 were killed or wounded and 335 horses were lost, for no ground gained and no strategic objective achieved. Contemporary and later military assessments are blunt about what happened: a failure of communication and command, not a triumph of tactics.

Alfred, Lord Tennyson published "The Charge of the Light Brigade" within weeks of reading the first newspaper account, and his poem did something the battle itself had not: it gave the disaster a clean, heroic shape. "Theirs not to reason why, / Theirs but to do and die" reframed blind obedience to a catastrophic order as a virtue in itself, and the phrase stuck so thoroughly that, more than a century and a half later, the charge is remembered first as a byword for reckless courage rather than for the command failure that caused it.

A Near-Disaster Remembered as a Turning Point

The Battle of Shiloh underwent a quieter version of the same transformation. On the morning of April 6, 1862, Confederate forces under General Albert Sidney Johnston achieved near-total surprise against Ulysses S. Grant's encamped Union army in southwestern Tennessee, driving Grant's men back for hours until thousands were pinned against the Tennessee River with nowhere left to retreat. Johnston was killed that afternoon, and only the arrival of reinforcements overnight allowed Grant to counterattack and reclaim the field on the second day. In the battle's immediate aftermath, Grant faced open calls in the press and in Washington for his removal, with critics pointing to the surprise itself — a serious command failure — as proof he was unfit for the job.

The version of the story that survived in popular memory is very different: Shiloh as the battle that proved Grant's toughness, sealed by a line supposedly delivered by Abraham Lincoln to Grant's critics — "I can't spare this man; he fights." The earliest documented source for that quote is an 1892 memoir by the journalist Alexander McClure, published three decades after the battle and long after Lincoln's death, and historians who have examined the claim note there is no contemporary record of Lincoln ever saying it. Whether or not the line is genuine, it did exactly what Tennyson's poem did for the Light Brigade: it gave a messy, nearly-catastrophic event a clean redemptive ending, retroactively, and that ending is the one that stuck.

Heroism Is Written, Not Just Fought

Set side by side, the two battles suggest the same pattern from opposite directions. A defeat caused by a command failure can be remembered as heroic sacrifice, if the right poem gets written fast enough. A near-defeat caused by another command failure can be remembered as a hard-won turning point, if the right story — even an invented one — gets attached to it afterward. In neither case does the retelling erase what the soldiers on the ground actually went through; it simply decides, for everyone who wasn't there, what that experience is supposed to mean.

Two Songs That Refuse the Clean Version

Bloody Hopes' "Into the Guns — The Light Brigade" and "Surrender, Swim, or Fight — A Shiloh Civil War Ballad" both stay closer to the ground-level reality than to the legend built on top of it: a valley with no way out in one case, a riverbank with three impossible choices in the other.
