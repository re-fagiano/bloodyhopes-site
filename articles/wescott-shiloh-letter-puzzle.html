M. Ebenezer Wescott’s Shiloh Letter: Eyewitness Account or Regimental Puzzle?

The soldier was real. The printed account is vivid. Standard institutional regimental chronologies put his unit somewhere else. Here is what the evidence proves—and what it still cannot explain.

Short answer: Wescott published a first-person Shiloh account in 1909, but the unit chronology and brigade organization examined here do not independently verify his presence in the battle. The conflict is real; its cause remains unresolved.

Research and editorial review: Bloody Hopes · Last revised August 26, 2026

This matters beyond one soldier’s biography. Historical art inherits the strengths and weaknesses of its sources; showing where a source stops being secure is part of using it responsibly.

What the 1909 Publication Establishes

The Library of Congress preserves a complete scan of M. Ebenezer Wescott’s privately printed Civil War Letters, 1861 to 1865: Written by a Boy in Blue to his Mother. Its introduction says that Wescott’s mother kept his wartime letters and later returned them to him. The surviving object online is the typeset 1909 book, not the original handwritten correspondence. We therefore know what Wescott chose to publish, but not whether every text reached print without correction, compression or combination.

The opening sequence is specific. A letter dated January 19, 1862, from Camp Randall says Wescott expects to join the 17th Regiment. A letter headed Pittsburg Landing, April 3, says “our regiment” left St. Louis aboard the steamer Imperial on March 26. A third, dated April 13, narrates the fighting of April 6–7 and reaches the sentence that inspired the Bloody Hopes ballad:

The printed words“There were three things to do, surrender, swim the river or fight to the death.”

The facsimile establishes the publication and its wording. It does not, by itself, authenticate every date, command relationship or battlefield position.

The Soldier and Company Were Real

Wisconsin’s official 1886 Roster of Wisconsin Volunteers lists “Westcott, Ebenezer”—with a final t—of Farmington in Company E, 17th Wisconsin Infantry. It records his enlistment on January 16, 1862, later status as veteran and corporal, and muster-out on July 27, 1865.

The same Company E roster names John McGourin as captain. That makes “Captain McGowen” in Wescott’s printed January letter a plausible phonetic, spelling or typesetting variant. These matches strongly establish Wescott’s identity and Company E association. They do not place him at Shiloh.

Where the Chronology Breaks

The National Park Service history of the 17th Wisconsin records the regiment at Benton Barracks until April 10 and moving to Pittsburg Landing on April 10–14. The Wisconsin Historical Society and Wisconsin Veterans Museum present the same sequence. Shiloh was fought on April 6–7.

This is not a disagreement over an hour or a landmark. It concerns the location of an entire regiment before and during the battle.

The Brigade Test

The April 3 text says there were “four other regiments” in Wescott’s brigade, but names only three: the 16th Wisconsin, 4th Kentucky and 20th Missouri, “besides ours,” under General John McArthur. That numerical gap is an internal warning in the printed account itself: a name may have been omitted, or the sentence may have been imperfectly remembered, edited or typeset.

At Shiloh, McArthur’s Second Brigade comprised the 9th and 12th Illinois, 81st Ohio, 13th Missouri and 14th Missouri. The brigade was dispersed during the battle: McArthur moved with the two Illinois regiments, while other elements served elsewhere. Lt. Col. Augustus L. Chetlain’s official April 10 report confirms the 12th Illinois acting with McArthur’s Second Brigade; commission-era organization records supply the complete five-regiment list.

The strongest concrete clue appears later. The 16th Wisconsin really did fight at Shiloh, in Everett Peabody’s First Brigade of Prentiss’s Sixth Division. After the battle, the newly arrived 17th joined the 16th, 21st Missouri and 25th Missouri in the same brigade. Quiner’s 1866 regimental history states that on May 24, during the advance on Corinth, that brigade was placed under General John McArthur.

Wescott’s printed April 3 description therefore resembles a real organization he would have known in May: the 16th and 17th Wisconsin together, Missouri regiments present, and McArthur in command. This makes retrospective conflation more plausible. It does not solve the case: the printed 4th Kentucky and 20th Missouri still do not match the actual May brigade, and a later organizational memory cannot by itself authenticate the April 6–7 narrative.

A Lead in the Names—not an Identification

The printed battle letter names John Thomas, Henry Thomas and Jack Case. Those names do not appear as such in the Company E roster of the 17th Wisconsin examined for this study. A Shiloh casualty list for Company E of the 16th Wisconsin does include Henry L. Thomas, killed on April 6. The overlap is worth recording because the 16th is already entangled in the brigade puzzle, but “Henry Thomas” is not a unique identifier. Without a manuscript, service file or other link, treating the two men as the same person would be speculation.

The Steamer Imperial

The vessel was real. In Our Army Nurses, Ruth Sinnotte recalled service aboard a hospital boat named Imperial operating between St. Louis and Pittsburg Landing. That independent account makes Wescott’s route plausible. No independently indexed transport or quartermaster record found in the web and digitized collections searched for this study, through August 26, 2026, connects the 17th Wisconsin to that vessel on March 26. This was not an exhaustive search of uncatalogued archival holdings. The ship proves the route existed, not that Wescott’s regiment travelled it on the stated date.

Has Anyone Already Solved It?

A targeted search of the publicly indexed web found the collection repeatedly reproduced, but no attempt to reconcile its Shiloh dates with the 17th Wisconsin chronology. A 2019 CivilWarTalk discussion presents the April letters as eyewitness testimony. A separate regimental thread gives the April 10–14 arrival and later recommends Wescott’s book, without connecting the incompatible claims. A family-history treatment and scholarly use of Wescott’s later Vicksburg writing likewise do not address the Shiloh problem.

This does not prove that no archivist or private researcher has investigated it. It means that no resolution was located in the accessible material searched for this article.

What May Have Happened

  1. One or more printed dates may be wrong.
  2. The 1909 edition may combine or revise material written at different times.
  3. Wescott may have moved separately under circumstances absent from the records examined.
  4. A summarized unit chronology may omit an exceptional movement—although the brigade mismatch would remain.

None is proven. Calling the letter a forgery would outrun the evidence. Calling it a verified eyewitness document from the 17th Wisconsin at Shiloh would do the same.

Editorial conclusionDescribe it as a Shiloh account published by Wescott in 1909, with an unresolved conflict in its regimental chronology.

What Could Resolve the Question

The strongest missing evidence would be Wescott’s compiled military service record, Company E muster rolls or morning reports for March–April 1862, transport records for the Imperial, and the original manuscript letters if they survive. Bloody Hopes welcomes a documented correction or archival lead through email or The Campfire.

From Document to Song

“Surrender, Swim, or Fight” takes its central phrase and emotional predicament from the account Wescott printed. The song is historical imagination, not authentication. Its depiction of a collapsing Union line pressed toward the Tennessee River belongs to the broader history of Shiloh; its attribution to Wescott retains the qualification established here.
