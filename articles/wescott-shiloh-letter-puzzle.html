M. Ebenezer Wescott’s Shiloh Letter: Eyewitness Account or Regimental Puzzle?

The soldier was real. The printed account is vivid. Standard institutional regimental chronologies put his unit somewhere else. Here is what the evidence proves—and what it still cannot explain.

Short answer: Wescott published a first-person Shiloh account in 1909, but the official chronology and Wisconsin’s own Shiloh commission contradict participation by the 17th Wisconsin as a regiment. Wescott’s individual presence is not independently verified, and the source of the conflict remains unresolved.

Research and editorial review: Bloody Hopes · Last revised August 26, 2026

This matters beyond one soldier’s biography. Historical art inherits the strengths and weaknesses of its sources; showing where a source stops being secure is part of using it responsibly.

What the 1909 Publication Establishes

The Library of Congress preserves a complete scan of M. Ebenezer Wescott’s privately printed Civil War Letters, 1861 to 1865: Written by a Boy in Blue to his Mother. Its introduction says that Wescott’s mother kept his wartime letters and later returned them to him. The surviving object online is the typeset 1909 book, not the original handwritten correspondence. We therefore know what Wescott chose to publish, but not whether every text reached print without correction, compression or combination.

The introduction contains a demonstrable chronological error: it says the letters run from January 1861, although the first printed letter is dated January 19, 1862 and Wescott enlisted that month. This does not explain the Shiloh discrepancy, but it establishes that at least one year was misstated in the 1909 edition. The introduction also says his mother returned the letters “about seventeen years” before publication, placing the manuscripts in Wescott’s possession around 1892; their later history has not been established.

The opening sequence is specific. A letter dated January 19, 1862, from Camp Randall says Wescott expects to join the 17th Regiment. A letter headed Pittsburg Landing, April 3, says “our regiment” left St. Louis aboard the steamer Imperial on March 26. A third, dated April 13, narrates the fighting of April 6–7 and reaches the sentence that inspired the Bloody Hopes ballad:

The printed words“There were three things to do, surrender, swim the river or fight to the death.”

The facsimile establishes the publication and its wording. It does not, by itself, authenticate every date, command relationship or battlefield position.

The Soldier and Company Were Real

Wisconsin’s official 1886 Roster of Wisconsin Volunteers lists “Westcott, Ebenezer”—with a final t—of Farmington in Company E, 17th Wisconsin Infantry. It records his enlistment on January 16, 1862, later status as veteran and corporal, and muster-out on July 27, 1865.

The same Company E roster names John McGourin as captain. That makes “Captain McGowen” in Wescott’s printed January letter a plausible phonetic, spelling or typesetting variant. These matches strongly establish Wescott’s identity and Company E association. They do not place him at Shiloh.

A second internal–external match is unusually specific. In the book’s final note Wescott identifies “Sam” as Samuel McClement, a schoolmate from the neighboring family farm who enlisted with him and remained his bunkmate. The official Company E roster records Samuel McClements, also of Farmington. This resolves the “Sam” mentioned in the opening letter and strengthens the attribution and biographical continuity of the collection. It is evidence for the writer’s identity, not for the location assigned to the April letters.

Where the Chronology Breaks

The National Park Service history of the 17th Wisconsin records the regiment at Benton Barracks until April 10 and moving to Pittsburg Landing on April 10–14. The Wisconsin Veterans Museum independently gives the same sequence, specifying departure on April 10 and disembarkation on April 14. Shiloh was fought on April 6–7.

This is not a disagreement over an hour or a landmark. It concerns the location of an entire regiment before and during the battle.

Shiloh is not confined to the two disputed April entries. In Letter XXIV, headed Camp Near Atlanta on July 24, 1864, Wescott compares a costly fifteen-minute action with what “we did at Shiloh.” Within the published collection, he therefore continued to treat Shiloh as a personal benchmark more than two years later, while the surrounding Atlanta narrative aligns closely with the 17th Wisconsin’s documented campaign. This makes a single isolated interpolation less satisfactory as an explanation. It still does not prove that the Shiloh reference appeared in an original 1864 manuscript: only the typeset 1909 edition has been examined.

The 1909 Wisconsin Commission Test

In the same year Wescott’s book appeared, the Wisconsin Shiloh Monument Commission published its official report. The commission had been created under Wisconsin law beginning in 1901, and its members were appointed from the 14th, 16th and 18th Wisconsin. Its report says that the commissioners chose one monument for “the three regiments” because all three had fought over the selected ground: the 16th and 18th on April 6–7 and the 14th on April 7.

This is stronger than silence in a general narrative. It is a dedicated, state-sponsored inquiry into Wisconsin participation, published contemporaneously with Wescott’s collection, and it recognizes no battlefield role for the 17th. Together with the regiment’s April 10–14 movement chronology, it supports a high-confidence conclusion that the 17th Wisconsin did not fight at Shiloh as a regiment.

The commission cannot by itself exclude every exceptional movement by an individual soldier: its subject was regiments, not a census of detached men. But Wescott’s text says “our regiment,” invokes his own captain, first lieutenant and comrades, and describes a brigade around them. Preserving it as an eyewitness account through an undocumented individual detachment would therefore explain only Wescott’s location, not the unit-scale narrative he printed.

The Brigade Test

The April 3 text says there were “four other regiments” in Wescott’s brigade, but names only three: the 16th Wisconsin, 4th Kentucky and 20th Missouri, “besides ours,” under General John McArthur. That numerical gap is an internal warning in the printed account itself: a name may have been omitted, or the sentence may have been imperfectly transcribed, edited or typeset.

At Shiloh, McArthur’s Second Brigade comprised the 9th and 12th Illinois, 81st Ohio, 13th Missouri and Missouri Sharpshooters then commonly designated the 14th Missouri. The brigade was dispersed during the battle: McArthur moved with the two Illinois regiments, while other elements served elsewhere. Lt. Col. Augustus L. Chetlain’s official April 10 report confirms the 12th Illinois acting with McArthur’s Second Brigade; the Shiloh National Military Park Commission’s organization study supplies the complete brigade list.

The two most anomalous names do not rescue the description. Dyer-derived Missouri unit histories report that the proposed 20th Missouri Infantry did not complete organization and is not documented as an operational regiment at Shiloh. “It never existed” would be too broad—an attempted organization may leave records—but it was not a functioning member of McArthur’s brigade. The 4th Kentucky Infantry that did fight at Shiloh was Confederate, in Robert P. Trabue’s brigade. The Union Kentucky regiments arriving with Buell’s Army of the Ohio did not include a 4th Kentucky Infantry in the Shiloh order of battle. If the two names were printed accurately, both are incompatible with the Union brigade described; a wrong number, state or later transcription remains possible.

The strongest concrete clue appears later. The 16th Wisconsin really did fight at Shiloh, in Everett Peabody’s First Brigade of Prentiss’s Sixth Division. After the battle, the newly arrived 17th joined the 16th, 21st Missouri and 25th Missouri in the same brigade. Quiner’s 1866 regimental history states that on May 24, during the advance on Corinth, that brigade was placed under General John McArthur.

Wescott’s printed April 3 description therefore resembles a real organization he would have known in May: the 16th and 17th Wisconsin together, Missouri regiments present, and McArthur in command. This makes retrospective conflation more plausible. It does not solve the case: the printed 4th Kentucky and 20th Missouri still do not match the actual May brigade, and a later organizational memory cannot by itself authenticate the April 6–7 narrative.

Names in the Battle Letter

The printed battle letter names John Thomas, Henry Thomas and Jack Case. Those names do not appear as such in the Company E roster of the 17th Wisconsin examined for this study. The Wisconsin Shiloh Monuments Commission’s casualty roll (pp. 43–44) includes Henry L. Thomas of Company E, 16th Wisconsin, among those killed at Shiloh. The match is weak, however: the roster associates that soldier with Hancock, while Wescott and Samuel McClements were from Farmington, and no personal or family connection has been found. “Henry Thomas” is not a unique identifier. The overlap is recorded as a name coincidence, not evidence that Wescott knew him or witnessed his death. John Thomas and Jack Case remain unidentified.

The Steamer Imperial

The vessel was real. In Our Army Nurses, Ruth Sinnotte recalled service aboard a hospital boat named Imperial operating between St. Louis and Pittsburg Landing. The U.S. Army’s postwar Medical and Surgical History of the War of the Rebellion places the Imperial among the hospital boats arriving after Shiloh, and Wisconsin’s official roster records a soldier dying aboard it on May 4. These sources independently establish the vessel and its immediate post-battle hospital role.

They do not establish the alleged March 26 movement. The documented post-battle role does not exclude earlier ordinary transport service, but no independently indexed manifest, newspaper report or quartermaster record found in the web and digitized collections searched through August 26, 2026 connects the 17th Wisconsin to the Imperial on that date. This was not an exhaustive search of uncatalogued holdings. The vessel’s existence cannot authenticate the regiment’s stated journey.

Has Anyone Already Solved It?

A targeted search of the publicly indexed web found the collection repeatedly reproduced, but no attempt to reconcile its Shiloh dates with the 17th Wisconsin chronology. A 2019 CivilWarTalk discussion presents the April letters as eyewitness testimony. A separate regimental thread gives the April 10–14 arrival and later recommends Wescott’s book, without connecting the incompatible claims.

A 2014 University of Southern Mississippi honors thesis likewise treats Wescott’s April account as eyewitness evidence in a study of soldiers’ mental states, without testing it against the 17th Wisconsin chronology. George C. Rable’s University of Alabama document is a useful index to the book, not an authentication study. Family-history and catalog records help establish Wescott’s identity and the publication’s circulation, but do not independently verify Shiloh. No modern source located in this review resolves the conflict; repeated modern citation is reception evidence, not corroboration.

This does not prove that no archivist or private researcher has investigated it. It means that no resolution was located in the accessible material searched for this article.

What May Have Happened

  1. One or more printed dates may be wrong.
  2. The 1909 edition may combine or revise material written at different times.
  3. Later brigade associations or another soldier’s experience may have been conflated with Wescott’s own chronology.
  4. Wescott may have moved separately under circumstances absent from the records examined.
  5. A summarized unit chronology may omit an exceptional movement—although the brigade mismatch would remain.

None is proven, and the possibilities are not equally supported. The unit-level question is now much firmer: every institutional source examined places the 17th outside the battle, while Wisconsin’s dedicated commission recognized only the 14th, 16th and 18th as its Shiloh regiments. What remains unresolved is whether Wescott moved exceptionally, remembered or adopted another experience, or published text whose dates or contents had changed. Calling the letter a forgery would outrun the evidence; calling it a verified eyewitness document would do the same.

Editorial conclusionWescott’s identity is confirmed. Participation by the 17th Wisconsin at Shiloh is contradicted at high confidence. His individual eyewitness status remains unsupported, and the origin of the printed discrepancy remains open.

What Could Resolve the Question

The strongest missing evidence would be Wescott’s compiled military service record; the service files of Captain John McGourin and Lieutenants Joseph F. Wigmore and Patrick Croghan; Company E muster rolls or morning reports for March–April 1862; pre-April 10 manifests or transport records for the Imperial; and the original manuscript letters if they survive. An inquiry covering these possibilities has been sent to the Wisconsin Veterans Museum Research Center. Bloody Hopes welcomes a documented correction or archival lead through email or The Campfire.

From Document to Song

“Surrender, Swim, or Fight” takes its central phrase and emotional predicament from the account Wescott printed. The song is historical imagination, not authentication. Its depiction of a collapsing Union line pressed toward the Tennessee River belongs to the broader history of Shiloh; its attribution to Wescott retains the qualification established here.
