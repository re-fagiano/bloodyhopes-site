Participant history · Virginia, July 21, 1861

The Battle That Could Not Recognize Itself

At First Bull Run, two unfinished armies tried to identify uniforms, flags, roads, orders and victory. The most dangerous mistake was often believing they understood what they saw.

How to read this article. Underlined words open the archive: hover or focus for witness, date and passage; click for the source. Contemporary reports establish actions, but they also defend reputations. Private letters restore bodily scale, but not omniscience. Later memoirs explain memory, not automatically the battle. Each claim below is kept beside the kind of evidence capable of supporting it.

The problem

An army is partly an agreement about appearances

In July 1861 that agreement did not yet exist. State units arrived in gray, blue, militia dress and improvised clothing on both sides. National flags resembled one another when folded by wind and smoke. Officers possessed maps without reliable movement, plans without trained staffs and soldiers who often knew their regiment better than their army.

On Henry Hill, Union artillery officer Charles Griffin saw an unidentified formation. The troops were Virginians in blue. A warning that they were Confederates was overruled; their volley struck at close range. The incident did not single-handedly decide the battle, but it condensed its central danger: recognition arrived after exposure.

Two names are already two memories

Union usage made the creek—Bull Run—the battle’s name. Confederate usage made the railroad junction and region—Manassas—the name. Neither is false. Each locates meaning differently: an obstacle crossed and recrossed, or a strategic place defended.

This article uses both. “Bull Run” and “Manassas” are not merely regional preferences; they are the first surviving evidence that the same event entered history from opposite directions.

The field before the story

A turning movement that spent its strength in arriving

McDowell’s plan fixed Confederate attention at the Stone Bridge and Blackburn’s Ford while a larger column marched north to cross at Sudley Ford and descend on the Confederate left. The idea was intelligible. Its execution required inexperienced troops to begin in darkness, follow crowded roads, preserve intervals and reach an unfamiliar ford on time.

The timetable was political as well as military. Washington wanted movement before the terms of ninety-day volunteers expired; McDowell knew his army was not ready. Urgency did not cause every later failure, but it helps explain why an unfinished force was asked to execute a complicated turning movement.

The battle was already being shaped beyond the map. Robert Patterson’s Union force had been expected to hold Joseph E. Johnston in the Shenandoah Valley. Johnston slipped away instead. Most of his roughly 10,000 men reached the Manassas area on July 20 and 21 by the Manassas Gap Railroad, joining Beauregard’s roughly 22,000. The concentration began before the battle and continued during it; “railroad reinforcement” was therefore a sequence, not one dramatic train arriving at the climax.

The march ran late. That delay gave Confederate observers time to detect the movement and commanders time to redirect brigades. It also spent Union bodies before the main fight: dust, heat, thirst and hours on the road became tactical facts. The armies nominally available—roughly 35,000 Union and 32,000 Confederate—were larger than the forces actually committed, about 18,000 on each side. Industrial movement had altered the battlefield faster than command could fully absorb it.

I · The Union mirror

A planned movement becomes a search for an ending

2–9 a.m.

Delay is the first enemy

The turning column started late, collided with other formations and took longer routes than expected. By the time leading troops crossed Sudley Ford, the plan had announced itself. McDowell still achieved local superiority, but his soldiers entered combat after the effort required merely to reach it.

Morning

Success looks complete from the wrong distance

Union troops drove back the Confederate left from Matthews Hill. To units seeing the withdrawal, the enemy appeared beaten. To Confederate commanders it was movement toward a stronger line on Henry Hill. A tactical success became dangerous when its observers mistook one phase for the result.

Early afternoon

The guns cannot identify their audience

Ricketts’s and Griffin’s batteries moved onto Henry Hill, close to infantry whose identity was disputed. The 33rd Virginia’s blue dress contributed to the fatal hesitation. Guns and supporting infantry fought over ground where weapons changed possession more than once. The episode turned recognition from an abstract problem into seconds in which firing—or refusing to fire—carried equal risk.

Late afternoon

Retreat becomes information

When Confederate pressure widened and Union formations began leaving Henry Hill, men farther back often learned the army’s condition by watching others run. Wagons, civilians and troops converged near the crossings and roads east. The NPS account describes the route to Washington jammed with incompatible traffic. Retreat became rout not everywhere at once, but wherever the sight of flight supplied the only believable order.

II · The Confederate mirror

An exposed flank discovers that survival can look like destiny

Morning

A signal flag makes the unseen attack visible

Confederate plans also exceeded their army’s ability to execute them. Beauregard and Johnston contemplated offensive movements against Centreville while McDowell was already turning their left. Captain E. Porter Alexander’s signal message—“Look out for your left”—translated distant observation into actionable warning. Technology did not remove uncertainty; it moved one fragment faster.

Late morning

Defeat crosses a hill and receives another name

Evans, Bee and Bartow’s commands fell back from Matthews Hill. On Henry Hill, Thomas J. Jackson’s brigade remained comparatively steady. Bee’s reported comparison to a stone wall became inseparable from Jackson’s identity, although the exact words, intention and circumstances survive through conflicting recollection. A battlefield nickname is therefore both event and later claim.

Afternoon

Reinforcement changes what yesterday’s map meant

The last elements from the Shenandoah entered a fight already underway. Edmund Kirby Smith’s brigade left the train, marched toward the firing and lost Smith wounded; command passed to Elzey. Together with Early’s brigade, these fresh formations struck toward the Union right. They did not produce one perfectly directed counterstroke, but their arrival changed the available weight and extended the Confederate line. Local pressure became systemic collapse.

Evening

Victory stops at the limit of the victor

The Confederate army held the field but lacked the organization to transform Union flight into destruction or a march on Washington. Victory was real: the invasion had failed, guns and prisoners were taken, and the battlefield remained Confederate. Yet the same exhaustion and inexperience exposed in Union defeat constrained Confederate pursuit. The winner recognized success more easily than it could exploit it.

Below the names of generals

What survived in personal writing

The individual sources do not average into a universal soldier. Their value is precisely that they refuse to.

Rank-and-file evidence survives unevenly. Literacy, preservation, family choices and later collecting determined which voices entered an archive. One witness below is known only by initials; another reached the field too late to fight; one watched much of the battle after an injury. Those limits are not defects to conceal. They locate what each man could actually know.

Union · Maj. Sullivan Ballou · 2nd Rhode Island

Expectation before experience—and a transmitted text

The celebrated July 14 letter attributed to Ballou is famous because death followed it, but its historical value lies in the future tense: duty, love, nation and possible sacrifice arranged before battle supplied any image of what those words would require. Mortally wounded on July 21, Ballou became one of the ways later Americans learned to feel Bull Run. The complete transmitted text should be read before its ending is allowed to explain every sentence.

No autograph manuscript is known. The Rhode Island Historical Society holds early manuscript copies, and scholars disagree over authorship. This article therefore attributes rather than certifies the text.

Confederate · W. B. C. Coker · letter, July 28, 1861

Victory returns to camp as appetite and rumor

Coker wrote from Camp Cutts near Manassas Junction a week after the battle. His account sits beside requests for medicine, mail, food and speculation about the army’s next movement. That mixture matters: “victory” did not suspend ordinary needs. The Digital Library of Georgia preserves the original within the Frances Marion Coker papers.

Union · “C. C. E.” · volunteer, 27th New York · July 29, 1861

The retreat is first a problem of bodies

A volunteer whose surviving newspaper letter is signed only “C. C. E.” remembered the approach as a hot, dusty march on a narrow woodland track. Men discarded blankets, haversacks and rations before reaching the fight. In retreat he saw wounded men left behind and a road filled by thousands “covered with dust and blackened with powder.” His sentence—“The sights that field presented I can’t describe”—marks the point where enumeration fails without making the witness omniscient.

The period transcription identifies the regiment and date but not the writer’s full name. The initials are retained rather than silently expanded.

Confederate · Pvt. John H. Crittenden · July 23 and 26, 1861

A useful voice with an incomplete chain of custody

Crittenden’s surviving transcription distinguishes what was seen from what was heard. On July 23 he warned his parents that he wrote wartime news “just as I hear it.” Three days later he described camping amid the battlefield, where unburied bodies made the air intolerable. The distinction between rumor and sensory evidence is valuable—but it does not repair the source’s incomplete provenance.

The transcription identifies Crittenden as a private and preserves a sequence of letters, but no institutional catalogue record or manuscript image has yet been located. It is retained as a disclosed, provisional witness and is not used to establish disputed tactics, identities or casualty figures.

Union · Charles Carroll Morey · 2nd Vermont

The elephant is a march as much as a battle

Morey’s correspondence after Bull Run dwelt on exhaustion, poor food and discontent. First combat did not necessarily produce revelation in the vocabulary later memorialists preferred. It could be remembered as a body depleted by marching and an army failing at care. His letters are discussed in the American Battlefield Trust’s study of the soldier’s battle experience.

Confederate · Pvt. James T. Petty · 17th Virginia, Company B · July 18–22

A private records rumor becoming friendly fire

Petty’s pocket diary catches information before a staff history can clean it. On July 18 couriers reported enemy losses “by scores & hundreds.” On July 19 he turned from rumor to a harder observation: a Confederate regiment had fired into Confederate troops, and this was, he wrote, the third such incident. During the battle he crossed Bull Run as a skirmisher, fell into waist-deep water, lost his gun and returned with another before an injury sent him back. The diary preserves confidence, confusion and bodily mishap in the same hand.

Confederate · Pvt. James S. Newman · 13th Virginia, Company C · July 19–22

Railroad reinforcement from inside the crowded car

“Reinforcement by rail” becomes less elegant in Newman’s diary. He waited through delays, rode through the night crowded “like sheep,” and reached Manassas on July 21 after nearly two days with almost no food. Marching toward the field through dust so thick he could not see five paces, he met wounded men giving incompatible messages: hurry, you are needed; you are too late, the work is finished. He arrived after the Union collapse. The next day, viewing the dead, he was disturbed by his own composure and wrote of the “hardining effects of active service”. His value is not combat heroism but proximity without participation: he saw the machinery of reinforcement and the field it failed to reach in time.

Civilian field · spectators, residents and involuntary witnesses

The audience enters the event it came to observe

Civilians did travel from Washington toward Centreville, including politicians and journalists. The familiar tableau of tranquil family picnics beside the fighting is an enlargement, not a reliable description of one organized crowd. What matters is stranger: nineteenth-century spectatorship shared roads with military logistics. When retreat began, carriages became obstructions and observers became participants in the army’s loss of movement.

Residents experienced the battle without choosing its spectacle. Confederate sharpshooters used the vicinity of the Henry house; Federal gunners, unaware civilians remained inside, fired to drive them out and mortally wounded the bedridden Judith Henry. Farms became positions, hospitals and burial ground. Weeks later Confederate lieutenant Lock Weems described dead men and horses still making the field physically present. The line between “civilian” and “battlefield” was not a safe boundary but a category the guns ignored.

The shared discovery

The first major battle did not reveal who would win the war. It revealed that neither side yet understood the instrument it had built.

The Union learned that manpower, railroads and national purpose did not automatically produce battlefield control. The Confederacy learned that courage, interior movement and victory did not automatically produce strategic decision. Both learned that volunteers could fight fiercely and still cease to function as armies when information, formation and confidence collapsed together.

The second battle · memory

What later generations made easier than it was

Documented civilians traveled toward Centreville; their carriages later obstructed retreat.

The picnic

The compact legend of fashionable families calmly dining beside combat makes hubris visible, but exaggerates the scene.

Jackson’s brigade stood while other Confederate formations rallied nearby.

Stonewall

Bee’s exact words and intention remain disputed; the nickname’s truth as memory exceeds what can be proven about one utterance.

Confederates held the field and stopped the Union advance.

A war nearly won

The victorious army was too disorganized and exhausted for the decisive pursuit later imagination demanded.

Union retreat became chaotic on constrained roads, especially where wagons and civilians converged.

Everyone ran

Regulars and organized rear guards covered withdrawal; “rout” describes a system collapsing, not identical conduct by every person.

What this reconstruction cannot prove

No surviving selection of literate witnesses can statistically represent every soldier, civilian, enslaved person or free Black resident affected by the campaign. Reports written after defeat defend decisions; reports written after victory organize contingency into merit. Letters repeat rumor and withhold what writers do not wish families to know. The method here is accumulation without forced unanimity: identify the witness, locate the moment, disclose the source type and refuse to make one fragment stand for the whole mirror.
