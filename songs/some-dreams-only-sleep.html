The song

A young Virginian marches under the language of home, inherited liberty and local belonging. Four years later his company fits beneath one tree. At Appomattox he stacks his rifle, accepts parole and walks back through a country that has defeated his army but cannot instantly rewrite his inner life.

A Human Voice Is Not a Historical Verdict

The narrator says “our fields,” “our county names” and “no distant hand.” Those are plausible forms of personal memory, but they do not explain secession by themselves. Confederate declarations and the Confederacy's constitution placed slavery, racial hierarchy and the protection of slave property at the center of the new state. Individual soldiers could fight from mixed motives—kinship, coercion, local loyalty, comradeship or belief—without changing the political purpose their government declared.

The song therefore asks the listener to inhabit defeat without accepting the defeated cause as just. Its title describes memory's persistence, not the moral legitimacy of the dream that survives.

What the Army Laid Down

Grant's terms required public arms, artillery and property to be stacked and surrendered, while officers retained side arms and private baggage. Men were paroled and allowed to return home if they obeyed the laws and did not take up arms again. Grant separately allowed men who claimed horses or mules to keep them for farming and arranged Federal rations for an army worn down by hunger.

On April 12, Confederate infantry marched by divisions through Appomattox Court House, stacked rifles and folded flags. The war continued elsewhere, but the Army of Northern Virginia ceased to exist as a fighting force.

What Is Documented—and What the Song Invents

Documented: Lee's retreating army was hungry and reduced by combat, capture and desertion; the April 12 ceremony involved stacked rifles and folded colors; parole passes enabled men to travel home and obtain Federal rations. The Confederacy's secession documents repeatedly identify slavery as a central cause.

Invented: this company, narrator, mother, loaf of bread and private dialogue. The claim that one nation “died” at Appomattox is poetic compression: other Confederate armies remained in the field, and the legal and political destruction of slavery required the Thirteenth Amendment and a contested Reconstruction.

“History remembers the fallen. Some dreams only sleep.”

We were boys when Virginia
called the counties into line.
Left the corn within the furrow.
Left our mothers at the gate.
We knew the tale of '76,
how a people broke a crown.
They wore no red. They served no king.
Still we thought it had come round.

We had no words for history.
We said our fields are ours.
We said no distant hand should choose
the road beneath our feet.

We spent the springtime of our lives
to keep the world we knew:
the farms, the graves, the county names,
the old words handed through.
Sworn to shadows,
we bent beneath their peace.
But history does not lose the beaten,
and some dreams only sleep.

By '65, our company
could stand beneath one tree.
The boys who marched away with us
were names in family Bibles.
No flour left, no leather,
no flag without a tear.
We still walked west because our homes
were somewhere at our rear.

Then cannon ceased. The order passed:
stack arms and fold the flags.
A stranger placed some bread in mine
and signed my road back home.

We spent the springtime of our lives
to keep the world we knew:
the farms, the graves, the county names,
the old words handed through.
At Appomattox, sworn to shadows,
we bent beneath their peace.
But history does not lose the beaten,
and some dreams only sleep.

You swore the Union kept men free.
We swore that freedom let us leave.
Four years decided which would stand.
They could not tell our hearts
what to believe.

We spent the springtime of our lives.
We gave them all we had.
They took the rifles from our hands
and sent us home beneath their flag.
One nation died at Appomattox.
The people still remained.
And the dream that could not follow us
went sleeping through our names.

The guns were still.
The road was long.
The fields were ours to reap.
History remembers the fallen.
Some dreams only sleep.
