Rum, Alabama, Rum!

A Comic Naval Ballad for the CSS Alabama

Active

Historical Context

The CSS Alabama was built in secret near Liverpool, England, launched under the cover name Enrica, and commissioned as a Confederate commerce raider on August 24, 1862. A hybrid steam-and-sail cruiser under Captain Raphael Semmes, she spent twenty-two months hunting Union merchant shipping across the Atlantic, the Caribbean, and the Indian Ocean, capturing roughly 66 vessels worth an estimated $6,000,000 — the most successful raiding career of any Confederate warship, and the only one to sink a Union warship in direct combat.

Her run ended on June 19, 1864, off Cherbourg, France, when Semmes sailed out to meet the waiting USS Kearsarge rather than complete repairs in dry dock. The battle lasted just over an hour before Alabama took a shell below the waterline and sank, ending one of the most notorious commerce-raiding campaigns in naval history.

A Comic Song for a Deadly Ship

Sailors on both sides of the blockade turned their fear and boredom into rum, gallows humor, and songs with more chorus than sense — a long tradition of naval life at sea for months at a time. "Rum, Alabama, Rum!" leans into that tradition deliberately: a bouncing, almost drinking-song melody wrapped around a ship that sank sixty-plus merchant crews' livelihoods and was, by any sober measure, one of the deadliest commerce raiders of the war. The gap between the tune's grin and the ship's body count is the point — exactly the kind of dark, functional humor real sailors used to get through a war at sea.
