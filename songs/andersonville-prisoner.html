The piece

A marching warning turns into prison testimony. Stragglers who pause for coffee and bacon risk capture; inside Andersonville, coffee disappears and foul air, bad water and starvation replace it. The recording sets selected lines of the surviving poem as a warning among prisoners, with dry irony rather than a lament.

A Poem in the Back of a Diary

Morale survives at the back of the diary of Frederic Augustus James, a Union sailor captured at Fort Sumter and later held at Camp Sumter, better known as Andersonville. James was the only known sailor to keep a diary there. He died at the prison in 1864; the diary and his letters are held by the Massachusetts Historical Society.

The poem was first published with the diary in 1973. Its roadside capture scene points to a soldier rather than James as author. The edition attributed it to “P. Whitney,” but no matching name appears on the roster of the 1st Massachusetts Cavalry. Historian Gary Morgan has proposed that the name may have been miscopied and could refer to Theodore P. Whitney, who died at Andersonville in August 1864. That is a reasoned identification, not a settled fact.

The “Pinelog Institution”

Camp Sumter opened in February 1864 near Andersonville, Georgia. Its stockade was built from pine logs around an exposed enclosure with a stream running through it. Rapid overcrowding, inadequate shelter, scarce food, contaminated water and disease produced catastrophic mortality. The poem compresses that material world into its bitterest joke: no coffee, but more than enough foul air and water to destroy a constitution.

This recording belongs to a different branch of the Bloody Hopes archive from the original fictional ballads. The recording preserves the wording of its selected lines rather than inventing a new narrator. Music and performance give the document another route out of the stockade while the page keeps the chain of evidence—and its uncertainty—visible.
