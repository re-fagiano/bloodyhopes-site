Historical Context

Before the Civil War, the American "good death" followed a set ritual: family gathered at the bedside, witnessed the dying person's final words, and judged their spiritual readiness to pass. The war shattered that custom on an industrial scale. Hundreds of thousands of soldiers died hundreds of miles from home, often anonymously, buried on the battlefields where they fell rather than in family plots. The isolation from relatives that had once been rare became the ordinary experience of dying in America.

Into that gap stepped the condolence letter. Comrades, chaplains, and field nurses took on the role of witness that a mother or wife could no longer fill, writing home to assure a family that their son or husband had died bravely, peacefully, and "willingly surrendering to death" — whether or not that was strictly true. These letters became one of the war's most quietly devastating literary forms: formulaic, gentle, and final.

A Town That Never Existed, and Every Town That Did

Farmington is not an invented battlefield town. It is the Town of Farmington in Washington County, Wisconsin, where the state roster placed Union soldier Morgan Ebenezer Wescott when he enlisted. Writing to his mother from near Atlanta on July 24, 1864, Wescott said that “we” had lost more men in a charge of less than fifteen minutes than at Shiloh. He then named local men killed, wounded, or missing and wrote: “There will be mourning in Farmington when the sad news reaches there.” The song takes its title, its fifteen-minute frame, and its movement from battlefield to waiting homes from that published letter. The Shiloh comparison is reported as Wescott’s wording, not accepted as a verified regimental statistic: the 17th Wisconsin’s official chronology places the regiment outside that earlier battle, an unresolved source problem examined separately.

The individual domestic scenes in the ballad are imagined, but the community, the casualties Wescott named, and the sentence at the center of the refrain come from his account. The letter survives in Wescott's 1909 collection Civil War Letters, 1861 to 1865, digitized by the Library of Congress. The song expands one soldier's words into a portrait of how casualty news moved through Civil War communities.

July 21, Not the Battle of Atlanta on July 22

The letter supplies its own sequence. Wescott says his regiment heard the Battle of Peachtree Creek on July 20 but was not engaged; that night it marched through Decatur; the following afternoon it charged the outer Confederate works. Only after describing that fifteen-minute action does he begin, “On the morning of July 22nd,” and recount the larger Confederate assault in which General James B. McPherson was killed. The charge that sent mourning back to Farmington therefore belongs to July 21, 1864, not to the Battle of Atlanta on July 22.

The National Park Service chronology for the 17th Wisconsin independently separates “Leggett’s or Bald Hill, July 20–21” from the “Battle of Atlanta, July 22.” That supports the date distinction, although the exact works crossed in Wescott’s brief description have not been independently identified here. The song does not name a battle because it is not about one. It is about the second half of every battle: the knock at the door, the unfamiliar handwriting on the envelope, and a town quietly rearranging itself around an absence.
