Historical Context

On June 18, 1815, Napoleon led roughly 72,000 French troops against Arthur Wellesley, Duke of Wellington's 68,000-strong Anglo-allied army, aiming to destroy it before a nearby Prussian force under Field Marshal Blücher could arrive to reinforce it. Four French assaults on the allied center failed to break through, and by evening — with Blücher's Prussians arriving to seal the outcome — Napoleon's army was in full retreat. The battle ended twenty-three years of near-continuous warfare between France and the rest of Europe and closed the door permanently on Napoleon's career.

Waterloo is remembered as one of history's most consequential single-day battles: a few hours of infantry, cavalry, and artillery combat that reset the political map of Europe for a generation.

Inside the Square

The defining tactical image of Waterloo's infantry combat is the square — a compact formation with bayonets facing outward, used to resist cavalry. Wellington's allied infantry endured repeated French mounted attacks, artillery fire, and skirmishers. The cavalry rode between and around the squares, but standard accounts do not identify an allied square actually broken by the massed French cavalry charges.

The song deliberately departs from that record. Its young French cavalryman reaches the British square closely enough to be killed by the narrator; the episode and both men are fictional. He is not evidence of a historical breach, but the imagined human consequence of an impetuous cavalry assault launched without the infantry and artillery coordination needed to break disciplined squares. His smile crosses the opposing edge between attacker and defender in an act of war the song portrays as brave, futile, and recklessly ordered.
