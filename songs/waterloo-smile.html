Historical Context

On June 18, 1815, Napoleon led roughly 72,000 French troops against Arthur Wellesley, Duke of Wellington's 68,000-strong Anglo-allied army, aiming to destroy it before a nearby Prussian force under Field Marshal Blücher could arrive to reinforce it. Four French assaults on the allied center failed to break through, and by evening — with Blücher's Prussians arriving to seal the outcome — Napoleon's army was in full retreat. The battle ended twenty-three years of near-continuous warfare between France and the rest of Europe and closed the door permanently on Napoleon's career.

Waterloo is remembered as one of history's most consequential single-day battles: a few hours of infantry, cavalry, and artillery combat that reset the political map of Europe for a generation.

Inside the Square

The defining tactical image of Waterloo's infantry combat is the square — a hollow rectangular formation of soldiers, bayonets outward on every side, that was the standard defense against cavalry charges. Wellington's infantry held these squares for hours under repeated French cavalry assaults, packed shoulder to shoulder in a formation that offered no way out and no way to see beyond the men immediately around you. It was some of the most claustrophobic, sustained combat of the era: deafening, blind to the wider battle, and entirely dependent on the man beside you holding his position.

The song's title imagines a single, human moment inside that wall of bayonets and smoke — a smile passed between two soldiers holding the same impossible line, small and almost absurd against the scale of the battle unfolding around them.
