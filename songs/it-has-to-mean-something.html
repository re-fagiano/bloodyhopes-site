Three Mornings After Lee's Surrender

Lee surrendered the Army of Northern Virginia to Grant on April 9, 1865. The formal implementation unfolded over several days: cavalry weapons on April 10, artillery on April 11 and the Confederate infantry's stacking of rifles and folding of flags on April 12. The song's “three mornings later” therefore points to the infantry ceremony, not to the meeting in the McLean House itself.

Grant's written terms allowed officers and men to return home on parole after public arms and property were surrendered. He also arranged 25,000 rations for Lee's hungry army. That documented institutional act stands behind the song's intimate image of one man placing bread in another's hands; the individual exchange is dramatic invention.

The Promise Borrowed from Gettysburg

The refrain echoes Lincoln's Gettysburg Address without quoting it mechanically. Lincoln asked the living to ensure that the dead had not died in vain and joined preservation of the nation to a “new birth of freedom.” The song turns that public language into a soldier's private test: ending battle is not enough if the rebuilt Union repeats the violence or abandons the freedom for which it claims the war was fought.

This is one perspective within the Appomattox pair. Some Dreams Only Sleep follows a defeated Confederate homeward. The companion study The Three Days at Appomattox keeps empathy for individual soldiers separate from approval of the cause for which the Confederacy seceded.

What Is Documented—and What the Song Invents

Documented: Lee's surrender on April 9, the April 12 stacking of arms, folded colors, hungry Confederate troops, Federal rations and parole passes that protected the journey home. Joshua Chamberlain later described a silent, respectful salute during the ceremony, but his account is retrospective and its precise choreography has been debated.

Invented: the narrator, the specific celebration, the seventeen-year-old, the shared loaf between two unnamed soldiers and every private exchange. “Rails through ashes” and “wheat standing high by June” compress Reconstruction into imagined signs of recovery.
