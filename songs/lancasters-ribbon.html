Historical Context

The ballad's regiment, platoon, and nineteen-year-old narrator are fictionalized rather than a reconstruction of a documented unit episode. Their setting draws on the real Peninsular service of the regiment then officially titled the 4th (The King's Own) Regiment of Foot. It landed in Portugal in 1808, joined Sir John Moore's retreat and the Battle of Corunna, returned to the Peninsula in 1810, and later fought at Badajoz, Salamanca, Vitoria, and San Sebastián. The later title “King's Own Royal Regiment (Lancaster)” did not exist during the campaign; the Lancaster designation arose from the regiment's later history.

Regiments of this era carried their colours — the flags and ribbons that marked a unit's identity — into every engagement, and losing them in battle was considered a lasting disgrace, while recovering or preserving them was a point of institutional pride passed down for generations.

A Ribbon Passed Down

Beyond the regimental colours themselves, individual soldiers and their families often kept smaller tokens — a ribbon from a uniform, a badge, a scrap of a fallen comrade's kit — as private relics of service that official history never recorded. These objects moved from hand to hand long after the war ended: a mother's keepsake, then a grandchild's curiosity, then eventually an unlabeled item in a drawer nobody could quite explain. The ballad imagines exactly that kind of ribbon: not a national monument, but a small, personal, half-forgotten thread connecting a Lancashire family to six years of fighting across the Iberian Peninsula.

Source and Fiction Note

The military chronology is based on the National Army Museum's regimental history. The named soldiers, ribbon, wedding promise, and circumstances of the death are inventions of the ballad.
