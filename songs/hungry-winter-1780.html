Historical Context

General George Washington chose Morristown, New Jersey, for the Continental Army's 1779–1780 winter encampment, a strategic position between New York and Philadelphia. Historians and the National Park Service describe that season as more severe than the better-known winter at Valley Forge. Contemporary weather observations and later reconstructions record repeated snowstorms and prolonged cold, but the often-repeated total of twenty-eight snowfalls should be understood as a modern historical count rather than a phrase drawn from one soldier's account.

Cold was only part of the crisis. Inflation, weak public credit, transport failures, and reluctance to accept Continental paper currency all disrupted supply. Exchange rates varied sharply by date and place, so “thirty paper dollars to one dollar of coin” is illustrative rather than a fixed rate for the entire encampment. Washington authorized requisitions from surrounding counties, with certificates intended as compensation, when ordinary purchasing failed to feed the army.

Counting the Nights

Soldiers' accounts from Morristown describe stretches of days when rations simply did not arrive — not reduced rations, but none at all — while snow kept supply wagons from reaching camp. "Four Days, Four Nights" names one such stretch: not a battle, not a single dramatic event, but the much quieter and more grinding ordeal of counting down the hours between one meal and the next uncertain one, in a war remembered far more for its battlefields than for the winters that nearly ended it before any battle could be fought.
