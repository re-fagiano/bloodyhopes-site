The song

At Gettysburg, a recruit hungry for glory bayonets a boy who has already raised his hands. The army gives him a medal; memory turns it into a wound he cannot outlive.

Historical Context

Gettysburg was the bloodiest battle of the American Civil War: three days of fighting in July 1863 left roughly 50,000 men killed, wounded, captured, or missing. Its scale has often been reduced to maps, turning points, and the climactic failure of Pickett’s Charge. This ballad looks elsewhere—at the intimate violence hidden inside the victory lists.

The central killing is a fictional, composite episode rather than a claim about a named soldier or a documented moment of Pickett’s Charge. Gettysburg supplies the historical world; the song asks what happens when the heroic script survives in public after it has collapsed inside the person ordered to perform it.

A Medal Worn Like a Wound

The raised hands are unambiguous: the enemy boy has stopped fighting. The narrator, still possessed by his dream of becoming a man through violence, drives the bayonet in anyway and then begs the victim to die quickly enough to resemble the clean deaths promised by stories.

His comrades call him brave. A major pins on a medal and privately names the truth: “You killed that boy. Congratulations.” The final verses follow the veteran home, where official honor cannot contain moral injury. The medal is wrapped like a bone, worn like a wound, and the last gunshot is his own—not a triumph, but the final consequence of a glory the song has dismantled.
