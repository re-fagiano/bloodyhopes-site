The song

At Gettysburg, a recruit hungry for glory bayonets a boy who has already raised his hands. The army gives him a medal; memory turns it into a wound he cannot outlive.

Historical Context

Gettysburg was the bloodiest battle of the American Civil War: the National Park Service estimates 51,000 men killed, wounded, captured, or missing across July 1–3, 1863. Its scale has often been reduced to maps, turning points, and the climactic failure of Pickett’s Charge. This ballad looks elsewhere—at the intimate violence hidden inside the victory lists.

The central killing is a fictional, composite episode rather than a claim about a named soldier or a documented moment of Pickett’s Charge. Gettysburg supplies the historical world; the song asks what happens when the heroic script survives in public after it has collapsed inside the person ordered to perform it.

A Medal Worn Like a Wound

The raised hands are unambiguous: the enemy boy has stopped fighting. The narrator, still possessed by his dream of becoming a man through violence, drives the bayonet in anyway and then begs the victim to die quickly enough to resemble the clean deaths promised by stories. This is not merely a modern rule projected backward. The Union Army’s April 1863 General Orders No. 100, the Lieber Code, treated soldiers who threw away their arms and asked for quarter as prisoners of war; Article 71 prescribed punishment for intentionally killing an enemy already wholly disabled.

The weapon is historically possible but statistically exceptional. A postwar summary based on the U.S. Surgeon General’s records counted 922 saber or bayonet wounds among 246,712 treated wounds—about 0.37 percent. That figure describes recorded Union medical cases, not every battlefield death, but it shows how rarely edged weapons produced the wounds that reached official treatment records. The original six-volume medical history is available through the National Library of Medicine; the tactical context is examined in From Line to Skirmish.

His comrades call him brave. A major pins on a medal and privately names the truth: “You killed that boy. Congratulations.” The final verses follow the veteran home, where official honor cannot contain moral injury. The term is used here as an interpretive framework rather than a retrospective diagnosis: the U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs distinguishes moral injury from PTSD and connects it to acts that violate deeply held moral beliefs. The medal is wrapped like a bone, worn like a wound, and the last gunshot is his own—not a triumph, but the final consequence of a glory the song has dismantled.

Sources and Evidential Limits

  1. National Park Service, “Gettysburg” battle summary, for the dates, forces, casualties and broad sequence of the battle.
  2. U.S. War Department, General Orders No. 100 (Lieber Code, April 24, 1863), especially Articles 49, 56, 60, 71 and 79 on surrender, prisoners and disabled enemies.
  3. U.S. Surgeon General’s Office, The Medical and Surgical History of the War of the Rebellion (1870–1888), the underlying federal medical record for wound-causation statistics.
  4. William F. Fox, Regimental Losses in the American Civil War, chapter 2 (1889), for the contemporary summary of 246,712 treated wounds and 922 saber or bayonet cases.
  5. U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs, “Moral Injury in Veterans of War”, for the modern interpretive concept and its limits.

Documented: Gettysburg’s scale and chronology, the Union Army’s 1863 rules protecting surrendered and disabled enemies, and the rarity of recorded saber and bayonet wounds. Invented for the ballad: the unnamed boy, the killing, the medal, the major’s words and the veteran’s later death. No part of the fictional episode is presented as an identified Gettysburg incident.
