The song

Russian soldiers keep one clean shirt through the march and put it on before battle. The cloth is neither parade dress nor a symbol of innocence: it is an ordinary object made solemn by expectation. French fires and music carry across the dark while the Russian line answers with silence, crossed fingers and a morning nobody can promise to survive.

Glinka's Night Before Borodino

Fyodor Glinka served in the Russian army in 1812 and later published Sketches of the Battle of Borodino in 1839. In his account, the evening was cold and damp. Officers put on clean linen; soldiers who had saved a white shirt “for an occasion” did the same. Glinka immediately denied the festive reading: these preparations were not for a feast.

The same passage contrasts faint Russian fires and “sacred silence” with brighter French fires, drums, trumpets, music and shouting. When vodka was offered on the Russian line, Glinka wrote that nobody moved. Older soldiers crossed themselves and prayed. At dawn, artillery broke the night apart.

Tolstoy Preserved the Image—and Changed It

Decades later, Leo Tolstoy gave the white shirts a second life in War and Peace. Pierre sees peasant militiamen working in white shirts and wearing crosses on their caps. Boris then tells Kutuzov that the militia have put on clean shirts to prepare for death.

Tolstoy is not independent eyewitness confirmation. War and Peace is a novel published long after the battle, and its scene reshapes historical memory for literary purposes. Glinka speaks broadly of officers and soldiers; Tolstoy concentrates the image on the militia and states the meaning—readiness for death—more explicitly.

What Is Documented—and What the Song Invents

Glinka supports the clean linen and saved white shirts, the dim Russian fires, the louder French camp, the refused vodka, older men crossing themselves and the violent arrival of dawn. He is a participant source, but also a retrospective author writing more than a quarter-century later. His scene should be read as remembered testimony, not a stenographic record of every soldier on the line.

The individual man opening a knapsack, the supply carts, the repeated sentence “We're ready to die, Lord,” and the precise sequence of speakers are the song's inventions. Glinka records a different prayer to the Mother of God. Nor does his passage say that an entire Russian battle line visibly fought in white outer shirts. The song combines his soldiers' saved garments with Tolstoy's white-shirted militia, then follows the cloth into smoke and earth.

Lyrics

They did not dress for parade.
They put on clean shirts for the morning.

Evening lay on Borodino,
cold and heavy on the ground.
Small fires burned along the line.
The men spoke low, or not at all.
From the bottom of his knapsack
one man drew a shirt of white,
kept from dust through all the marching,
saved until a day like this.

Vodka passed along the fires.
An old man let the cup go by.
He crossed himself with blackened fingers:
“We're ready to die, Lord.”

Not for a feast, not for a bride,
not for the bells in town.
They put the clean white shirts on
before the guns came down.
No gold, no speech, no battle cry,
no promise they'd survive.
They put the clean white shirts on
and waited for daylight.

Officers changed their linen
where the supply carts stood.
Those who had a clean shirt with them
put it on as best they could.
Across the fields French fires burned bright,
and brass rang loud from hill to hill.
Their songs came riding through the night.
On the Russian line, all was still.

Some bore crosses on their caps.
Some only bowed their heads.
One old soldier faced the darkness:
“We're ready to die, Lord.”

Not for a feast, not for a bride,
not for the bells in town.
They put the clean white shirts on
before the guns came down.
No gold, no speech, no battle cry,
no promise they'd survive.
They put the clean white shirts on
and waited for daylight.

At dawn they stood upon the rise,
white showing through the gray—
not saints, not ghosts, not men without fear,
only men who chose to stay.
One clean shirt against the skin.
Cold light touched the cannon bore.
One low prayer ran down the line:
“We're ready to die, Lord.”

Not for a feast, not for a bride,
not for the bells in town.
They put the clean white shirts on
before the guns came down.
By noon the white was smoke and earth.
By dusk no cloth was clean.
They wore their clean white shirts to death
on Borodino field.

They had one clean shirt left.
They wore it to the guns.
Morning took the colour.
The earth kept everyone.
