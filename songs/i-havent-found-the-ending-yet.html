Cowpens and the Royal Fusiliers

The Battle of Cowpens took place on January 17, 1781, during the war in the southern colonies. Daniel Morgan defeated Banastre Tarleton's force. The 7th Regiment of Foot, the Royal Fusiliers, was among the British units present. That documented setting anchors the imagined correspondence.

The ending that cannot arrive

The recording's published description says that by the time she finishes a letter, he has been dead for three months. The locked south room and revised dates stretch waiting across years. The final notification reveals that his death has travelled home for three months while she is still imagining his return. Domestic objects carry the story: keeping his place is both an act of love and a repetition that cannot change the battle's outcome.

History and narrative invention

The regiment and battle are historical. The two people, their letters and their household are fictional: the recording claims no name, service record or grave. The three-month notification delay and the War Office-marked letter belong to the narrative, rather than a documented case. The song should be read as an imagined human cost within a real defeat.

Published description

She keeps his room, his books, his place at the table — writing him letters to tell him to come home. By the time she finishes one, he has already been dead three months.

Historical anchor: the letter names the 7th Regiment of Foot (Royal Fusiliers) and the Battle of Cowpens, January 17, 1781 — part of the British campaign in the Carolinas during the American Revolutionary War. Tarleton's force was nearly destroyed there, with roughly 55% of his men killed, captured, or missing. The regiment and the battle are historical; the two people in this song are not — no name, no service record, no grave is claimed. This is an imagined life built on a real and documented catastrophe, not a biography.
