Historical Context

Beginning in the mid-1600s, Africans enslaved on Suriname's coastal plantations escaped into the interior rainforest and formed autonomous Maroon communities. Different peoples and campaigns must not be collapsed into one war: the Ndyuka and Saamaka secured treaties with the Dutch colonial government in 1760 and 1762, while Boni became leader of the Aluku in 1765 and their major conflict with colonial forces followed from 1768 into the 1790s. The Aluku were not parties to the earlier treaties.

Dutch colonial forces repeatedly mounted punitive expeditions into the forest, destroyed settlements, and tried to recapture fugitives. The treaties of the 1760s recognized the freedom and territorial autonomy of particular Maroon peoples in return for peace obligations, but they did not end Maroon resistance as a whole. The Aluku under Boni continued fighting and were not brought into a formal peace agreement with the colonial authorities until 1860. The ballad combines experiences from this longer history; it should be heard as a composite narrative, not as the record of a single expedition or treaty.

Sources

Nationaal Archief, “Marrons: de Boni-oorlogen”; Inter-American Court of Human Rights, Moiwana Community v. Suriname.

A Debt Measured in Wounds

Decades of raid and reprisal on both sides — plantation attacks answered by forest expeditions, each side keeping its own ledger of what had been taken and what had been paid back — left a war with no clean beginning and no single decisive battle, only an accumulating balance of harm that both sides eventually recognized could only be settled by treaty rather than by victory. The song's title names that ledger directly: an exchange the two sides eventually agreed to stop keeping, not because either forgave the other, but because both had run out of blood to spend on it.
