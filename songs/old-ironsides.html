Why Was USS Constitution Called “Old Ironsides”?

USS Constitution is a United States Navy frigate launched in 1797, still today the oldest commissioned warship afloat in the world. She earned the nickname "Old Ironsides" during the battle of August 19, 1812, against the British frigate HMS Guerriere: according to the account passed down by her crew, enemy cannonballs appeared to bounce off her thick oak hull instead of piercing it, inspiring the image of an "iron" ship. The victory carried enormous symbolic weight for a still-young United States Navy, against the British naval power that then dominated the world.

Life at Sea

Beyond the battles, daily life aboard Age of Sail warships was marked by everyday hardships often more lethal than the enemy: drinking water stored in wooden casks for weeks or months turned foul, murky and at times dangerous to drink — a problem documented on virtually every navy of the era before the spread of better preservation methods. The Bloody Hopes ballad deliberately shifts its gaze from the glory of naval battle to this less celebrated but equally real side of war at sea: the quiet endurance of hunger, thirst and monotony, not only of the enemy.

This is a debunked tall tale
The ship, battle and nickname are historical. The inventory is not.

The exact quantities of water, rum and wine—and their mysterious disappearance—come from a modern comic legend, not USS Constitution’s log. The USS Constitution Museum shows that the dates, itinerary and alcohol totals do not withstand comparison with the historical record.
